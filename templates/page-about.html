01 / Background

The work changes.
The responsibility stays.

I’m Robert DeVore, a software engineer, security researcher, open-source developer, and technical writer. I’ve spent more than 20 years building commercial products, publishing systems, and tools that help people do their work.

I started with the web: PHP, WordPress, and the practical problems of getting a site into someone’s hands. Plugins became products. Products brought maintenance, support, integrations, and the responsibility of keeping other people’s businesses running.

My background includes independent development, commercial product ownership, and agency work for client organizations: custom engineering, security audits, performance optimization, and ongoing support. That experience shapes how I think about architecture and technical leadership. A system has to make sense to the people who will operate it, change it, and inherit it.

Today, my work spans programming language development, developer tooling, and AI-native software engineering. Kujo brings many of those interests together. The question underneath all of it is familiar: can we understand this system, verify what it does, and keep it useful beyond launch?

02 / Security research

I don’t just build software.
I find where it breaks.

Building and investigating software are closely connected. My research asks what happens when an input crosses a trust boundary, a permission check is missing, or a feature behaves differently from what its author intended.

My published disclosures cover WordPress plugins, from cross-site scripting to authorization failures. I report findings through coordinated disclosure programs so maintainers can address them and users have a public record to consult.

05 / Engineering principles

Leave the system
easier to understand.

Make the boundaries explicit.
Know what an input can affect, which permissions an action needs, and where a person should stay in control.
Prefer evidence to assumptions.
Keep the inputs, checks, and results close enough that someone else can reproduce the work.
Choose clarity that lasts.
A smaller, understandable system is easier to secure and maintain. Documentation and useful errors belong in the design.
Build for the next person.
Good interfaces, open source, and honest handoffs help work remain useful after its original author moves on.
